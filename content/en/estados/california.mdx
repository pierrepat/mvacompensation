California has more registered vehicles and more car accidents than any other state. It also has some of the most victim-friendly accident laws in the country — with one major exception that can devastate uninsured drivers.

California uses **pure comparative negligence**, meaning you can recover compensation even if you were mostly at fault. But under **Proposition 213**, uninsured drivers cannot recover pain and suffering damages — often the largest part of a settlement.

## Pure Comparative Negligence — The Most Favorable System

California's pure comparative negligence doctrine (established in *Li v. Yellow Cab Co.*, 1975; California Civil Code Section 1714) is the most favorable for accident victims:

- You can recover compensation **even if you were 99% at fault**
- Your recovery is reduced by your fault percentage, but never eliminated
- No threshold or cutoff

Example: $200,000 in damages at 40% fault = $120,000 recovery. At 80% fault = $40,000. At 99% fault = $2,000.

This is significantly more favorable than Texas (51% bar) or Florida (51% bar post-HB 837).

## Proposition 213 — The Trap for Uninsured Drivers

Prop 213 (California Civil Code Section 3333.4) strips uninsured drivers of the right to recover **non-economic damages** — pain and suffering, emotional distress, loss of quality of life.

You can still recover **economic damages**: medical bills, lost wages, property damage. But non-economic damages are often the largest portion of a settlement in serious injury cases.

This applies even if the other driver was **100% at fault**. It's not punishment for the accident — it's punishment for driving without insurance.

California offers a **Low-Cost Auto Insurance Program (CLCA)** for low-income drivers, with premiums starting around $250/year. **AB 60** allows undocumented individuals to obtain a driver's license, making insurance accessible.

## Statute of Limitations in California

- **Personal injury:** **2 years** (CCP Section 335.1)
- **Property damage:** **3 years** (CCP Section 338)
- **Wrongful death:** **2 years** from the date of death
- **Government claims:** Must file within **6 months** (Government Code Section 911.2)

The 6-month government claim deadline is critically short. If your accident involved a state vehicle, city bus, Caltrans, or any government entity, contact a lawyer immediately.

## Average Compensation in California

California juries tend to award higher than the national average due to the state's higher cost of living and medical costs. No cap on personal injury damages in auto accident cases.

## California Insurance Minimums

California's **15/30/5** rule is among the lowest in the country:

- **$15,000** per person bodily injury
- **$30,000** per accident bodily injury
- **$5,000** property damage

$5,000 in property damage barely covers a fender bender. UM/UIM coverage is strongly recommended — about 16% of California drivers are uninsured.

## Steps to Take After an Accident in California

1. **Call 911.** California requires reporting accidents with injuries or death.
2. **Document the scene.** Photos and video of all damage and conditions.
3. **Exchange information.** Required under California Vehicle Code Section 16025.
4. **Get medical attention promptly.**
5. **File SR-1 with the DMV within 10 days** if there are injuries, death, or $1,000+ in damage. This is your responsibility — separate from the police report.
6. **Notify your insurance.**
7. **Consult a lawyer** — especially important if liability is disputed or injuries are significant.

## Protections for Immigrants in California

California has the strongest protections in the country for immigrant accident victims:

- **SB 54 (California Values Act):** Limits local law enforcement cooperation with immigration authorities. Police responding to your accident will not contact ICE.
- **Evidence Code Section 351.2:** Prohibits courts from asking about or admitting evidence of immigration status in civil cases.
- **AB 60:** Allows undocumented individuals to obtain driver's licenses.
- Insurance companies **cannot ask** about immigration status when processing claims.

Your immigration status is legally irrelevant to your personal injury case in California.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Driving without insurance** — Prop 213 strips your right to pain and suffering damages, even if the other driver was 100% at fault.
2. **Not filing SR-1 with DMV** — You have 10 days. Failure can result in license suspension.
3. **Talking to the other insurer** — California insurers are aggressive, especially in competitive markets like LA.
4. **Settling before Maximum Medical Improvement (MMI)** — Never accept a settlement until your doctor confirms you've recovered as much as you will.
5. **Social media activity** — Insurers monitor all platforms.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I file a claim without insurance?**

Yes, but Prop 213 limits you to economic damages only (medical bills, lost wages, property). You lose the right to pain and suffering.

**Can I sue if I was mostly at fault?**

Yes — California's pure comparative negligence lets you recover even at 99% fault. Your award is reduced proportionally.

**How much does a California lawyer cost?**

Contingency fees, typically 33% to 40%. Nothing upfront. You only pay if they win.

**Does immigration status affect my case?**

**No.** California Evidence Code Section 351.2 bars courts from inquiring about immigration status in civil cases. Your attorney cannot share your information.

**What about Uber/Lyft accidents?**

Rideshare companies carry $1,000,000 in liability while a passenger is in the vehicle. Determining which policy applies can be complex — consult a lawyer.

**How long do cases take?**

Simple: 4 to 8 months. Moderate: 8 to 18 months. Serious/trial: 1 to 3+ years. LA County courts have heavier caseloads.
